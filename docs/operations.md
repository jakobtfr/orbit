# What NASA Actually Does Today

Researched 2026-11-14. This is the current operational baseline, not a hypothetical.

## The short version

ISS healthcare is **ground-centered and real-time**. A flight surgeon on console in Houston, backed by a biomedical engineer and a large specialist network, watches the crew and makes the calls. The crew's onboard medical lead is usually **not a physician** — often an astronaut with roughly **40 hours of medical training**. On Mars, that model breaks.

## Who is involved

| Role | What they do |
| --- | --- |
| **Flight Surgeon (FS)** | Console position `SURGEON` in MCC-Houston. Owns medical decisions, runs private medical conferences, on call 24/7. |
| **Crew Surgeon / Deputy** | Assigned to each increment; manages medical care pre-flight through post-landing; supervises biomedical data collection and in-flight countermeasures. |
| **Biomedical Engineer (BME)** | Sits next to the FS. Downlinks physiological, exercise, radiation, and environmental data; produces the daily medical status report; schedules medical activities. |
| **Crew Medical Officer (CMO)** | A crew member, usually a non-physician astronaut with ~40 h of medical training. Two are designated per increment. Performs in-flight physicals, runs the medical checklist (JSC 48522), manages onboard medical records. |
| **Integrated Medical Group (IMG)** | The virtual single medical team across NASA, ESA, JAXA, CSA, and Russian partners. |
| **ASCR** | Strength, conditioning, and rehab specialists who prescribe individual exercise. |

## What happens on a normal mission

- **Weekly private medical conferences (PMCs)** with each crew member. Private audio/video, not recorded into the mission loop. Details are not reported to Mission Control unless they could affect the mission.
- **Private psychological conferences (PPCs)** on a separate cadence.
- **Periodic health status (PHS)** and periodic fitness exams.
- **Pre- and post-EVA health assessments**; ground medical clearance before every EVA.
- **Daily medical interviews/check-ins** and a daily medical status report produced by the BME.
- **Data downlinked:** physiological data, exercise data, radiation dosages, medical exam results, and environmental health data (air, water, acoustics).
- **Privacy is designed in:** medical communication is treated as private under the Privacy Act; the FS has a separate private loop.

## Countermeasures already in the plan

| Area | Current practice |
| --- | --- |
| **Exercise** | ~2–2.5 h/day: ~1.5 h resistance (ARED) + ~1 h aerobic (treadmill or cycle). Crew now average about 2 h/day. |
| **Sleep** | 8.5 h scheduled sleep opportunity, plus 2 h pre-sleep and 1.5 h post-sleep. Actual ISS sleep averages **6.09 h**; 44% of nights are under 6 h. |
| **Orthostatic intolerance** | Fluid loading with 24–48 oz NaCl-water before deorbit; compression garments (anti-G suit ~75 mmHg; gradient garments ~55/30/20 mmHg). |
| **Radiation** | Personal dosimetry and autonomous neutron monitoring (CANS); career dose tracking. |
| **Environment** | CO₂ limited to 3 mmHg 1-hour average; air, water, acoustic, and microbial monitoring. |
| **Behavioral health** | Psychological support programs for crew, families, and ground personnel; private psychological conferences. |
| **Post-flight** | Immediate medical evaluation, daily health status exams, rehab. |

## How often people actually get sick

From a review of 46 long-duration ISS crew members (20.6 flight years):

- **3.40 clinical events per flight year**, about **1.7 per 6-month expedition**.
- **46% of crew** had at least one "notable" event (lasting >1 week, recurring, or not responding to treatment).
- Most common: **skin rashes (1.12/flight-year)** and **upper respiratory symptoms (0.97/flight-year)**.
- Events cluster in the **first month** of flight.
- No forced medical evacuations from ISS to date (as of the cited 2008 source).

## The exploration gap

NASA's Exploration Medical Capability (ExMC) element states:

- Over **100 spaceflight-relevant medical conditions** have been identified.
- One-way communication delays can exceed **20 minutes**; evacuation and resupply become impossible.
- The crew will need to **detect, diagnose, treat, and prevent** events autonomously.
- Their solution direction is **Earth-Independent Medical Operations (EIMO)** with an in-vehicle **clinical decision support system (CDSS)**.
- A CDSS is expected to raise the crew's effective scope of practice. In a 26-month reference mission simulation, raising cognitive and procedural scope of practice to level 5 reduced deaths by at least 34%.
